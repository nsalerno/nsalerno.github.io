About Me

My name is Nicholas Salerno and I program computers. I have been doing so since 1991 (although my first exposure to computers was in 1986). In 2001 I graduated from the Rochester Institue of Technology and have been working in the software industry since. My hobbies outside of computers is music and games. I play piano and guitar, although not so much as of recent years. I was very good at my peak and had excellent dexterity. An example would be my favorite of Beethoven’s works the Sonata Pathétique. I had to learn how to play it and I eventually did (after much days and hours of practice). Outside of music I enjoy playing games, all sorts of games. From board games, to table-top, to cards, and to video games. My favorite is chess and I still play it today, albeit it leisurely (I don’t study the game like I did in my younger years). I also like learning about game theory, which like computer and software theory I cannot get enough. I’ve always enjoyed learning the theory behind the practice. If there is one thing I learned in life is that while advanced techniques are nice and sometimes needed, basics and first principles are a must.

Computers

RIT was an awesome experience for me. Before college I’ve only really known the PC (at the time known as something that was IBM compatible). At college I was finally exposed to all sorts of different computers, software, and (most importantly) ideas. College was also the place where I finally got to be with like-minded people, some of whom were way smarter than me. I was also an introvert at the time and these same people put in the effort to get me out of my shell. While I have lost touch as a function of time and geographic distance, I am forever indebted to these people for transforming me into a better person.

My official title in life is “software engineer.” That said, I am and always will be the humble computer programmer. I have done all kinds of computer programming. From low level assembly language (x86), to procedural (BASIC, C, Pascal), to objected-oriented (C++, Eiffel, Java, Delphi, C#), up to the highest-level scripting languages (JavaScript, Python, TypeScript). The only real paradigm I’ve yet to do, in a practical or professional capacity, is the functional paradigm. One of these days I would like to tackle that as I’ve read many wonderful things about Lisp, Haskell, Scala, Forth, and so on. Yes, I have written some LISP programs in college but that doesn’t count. I never wrote a practical application nor used it on the job. There’s a difference between writing a LISP program that processes binary trees (extremely easy and few lines of code) and actually writing a program that implements real use-cases of an end user.

Career

I am fortunate that my professional career had moments of being around great people and working on great software. I’ve had the honor and privilege of working with excellent software engineers, which made me a better software engineer. It also gave me an opportunity to work on interesting software that was challenging. These was good times as I felt I was being paid to have fun.

I’ve also had some regular jobs, where the projects are not so interesting. That is OK and is part of life. Sometimes you get to have fun and sometimes you do the needful that makes the world go round. Still, though, there is a challenge to be found in places you would least expect. Even in the lesser jobs were hidden challenges that helped me become a better software engineer.

And there, in a nutshell, is all about me.
