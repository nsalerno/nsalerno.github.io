I am a coder

In the beginning

I am a coder. When I was in the third grade the school I attended had a computer class as part of the curriculum. In this class they taught Logo. Well, not really teach. The teacher showed what to type, we typed it into the computer, saw the results, and got a passing grade. Anyways, here I am entering the following commands:

FD 50
RT 90
FD 50
RT 90
FD 50
RT 90
FD 50

I run the program and a square appears before my eyes. I’m made to understand that I have instructed the computer to draw pixels fifty units in a foward direction, make a right turn, draw pixels again (fifty units), make another right turn, repeat until we complete the square. I was hooked immediately.

Fast forward to today. Nothing has really changed for me. I write instructions that make a computer, or any programmable machine, do something of interest. To this day the gratification is still the same. However, it’s not that simple of course. I don’t just write instructions. Along the way I’ve learned about the art and science of coding. I define this to mean everything involved to transform what we know in our own language and thoughts into something the computer understands so that the computer does what we want. This means transforming natural phenomenon and/or action into a series of data structures and algorithms that simulate what we want (as well as transforming any output back into something we understand). This is what it means to me to be a coder, to encode to the computer and decode from the computer.

When I learned about how applications and games worked, I didn’t expect that it was about choosing a data structure that in no way mimicked our mental models of reality. I didn’t expect that it was a series of clever hacks, tricks, and outright cheats that make the computer simulate what we want. For example, when we see a simple video game with a continuous scrolling background we think of a roll of paper that is being rotated with a small section of the paper in the viewport of the display. But that is not how it works in the software. Instead, tricks are being employed to create the illusion of a background scrolling by us as our hero progresses through the level. There is tiling, identification of areas that are dirty vs. not dirty, clever reuse to avoid work, employing a feature of the video card to change the start of scanning, caching techniques, etc. If I had to pick an article that best exemplified what it means to be a coder and the practice of encode to the computer, decode from the computer, it would be this post about how Pitfall works, specifically how a single byte is used to describe all 255 areas and the algorithm used to navigate between the areas such that all areas can be explored.

The journey and the destination

Part of the thrill of programming computers is the act itself, the writing of the code. In life there is the journey and there is the destination. Some people enjoy the journey. Some people enjoy the destination. Both are OK, there is nothing inherently wrong with either perspective. For a chef, the act of choosing the ingredients, crafting the flavor, and doing the cooking is all part of the journey. The destination is the completed dish. Once the destination is reached there is a joy in seeing the fruits of one’s labor. For a customer at a restaurant there is no journey. The customer is hungry and just wants the completed dish as soon as possible. For the customer it’s only about the destination. The ideal world for the customer is the magic technology of any sci-fi TV show. You ask for a Beef Wellington and the technology produces it for you instantly in two seconds. Let me use another example closer to reality. Your sales department wants software that has features that would sell in the market. Your sales department is only interested in the destination. They don’t care about the journey of designing the software and then building the software. They simply want the product now so that they can begin selling it to customers. None of these perspectives are wrong.

I am a coder. I enjoy both the destination and the journey. Don’t misunderstand me, of course I want to complete the software project. I want to see it in the hands of the customer. I want to see that it makes other people become productive in their tasks. But, part of the enjoyment for me is the designing and building of the software. The act of going on the journey has and always will cause endorphins to be released in my brain.

Many developers love to work on greenfield projects (what I call the version 1.0). Once the project is complete, they look to move onto other greenfield projects. These same developers usually disklike brownfield projects (what I call the version 2.0). You know what I love doing? I love working on version 2.0 of the project (with the assumption that I was part of the 1.0 endeavor). Why? I’m not sure, perhaps it’s the way my brain is wired. What I will tell you is that I very much enjoy taking the lessons of 1.0 (what worked well, what worked poorly, what were the pain points, how would we do it differently) and using those lessons to make version 2.0 be awesome. I am not eager to work on many greenfield projects because I’ll only have a portfolio of all sorts of mistakes made and bad ideas implemented. I’ve had the privilege and honor to work with people who were insanely smart. These people had an unnatural ability to make 1.0 correct from the start. I am not that person, unfortunately. I am a regular person like anyone else. My version 1.0 works but has issues, my version 2.0 is much better, and my version 3.0 makes me look like a genius. That’s how it has always been for me. I don’t have the ability to cut short the journey, but regardless I enjoy making the trip.

English as a programming language

I am a coder. That means I take the requirements written in English (because I am an English-speaking person) and transform it into the code that the computer understands. I use a programming language to achieve this. The programming language is a middle ground between English and machine code. It is English-like but still very much computer instructions where we understand what each line of code means to the CPU.

Not every programming language is equal. Some programming languages are better than others (given a context or given the nature of the problem to solve). The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis states that the language you speak shapes how you think. My interpretation of that is the better the language the better you can understand the problem and solve it. As the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote: “The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.” I have found this to be so true in the field of computer science. If all a programmer knows is a low-level systems programming language that programmer sees everything as memory blocks with raw pointers to specific locations and pointer arithmetic as a means to get things done. Now, don’t get me wrong, anyone who is smart enough and determined enough will get things done in any language, even if they have to move a lot of Earth. However, we see that there is a significant difference between a low-level programmer and a high-level programmer when the topic at hand is a domain-specific language. The high-level language allows you to think in terms of the domain (domain complexity) while the low-level language saddles you with always knowing about how the computer works (accidental complexity). For example, you want to add another data item to your data collection but, uh-oh, the memory buffer is full and you are on the hook for handling the accidental complexity of finding more memory space for your data collection, as well has handling the ramifications of how you found the extra space.

I don’t mean to demonize the low-level systems programmer because sometimes too much abstraction can have adverse performance to the point the software is unusable. We see this with high-level scripting languages where the programmer blissfully uses a data structure without a care in the world because the data structure knows how to grow and maintain itself. The programmer can focus purely on the domain area. However, the performance suffers because the computer is not being used in an optimal fashion. The low-level programmer understands the accidental complexity and knows how to inject the accidental solution into the code so that it performs well. The price is that the source code isn’t pure in terms of the language of the domain, which may hurt portability. It’s a trade-off and a balancing act. But, in no way is the low-level programmer making the software worse off, quite the opposite, the low-level programmer is getting the software done and usable.

I have an appreciation for the low-level languages (these days I work with high-level languages but I too had used low-level languages to great effect). There is nothing inherently wrong with using low-level languages. If you are going to write an operating system kernel you are going to use C and/or Rust because they are the best fit for the problem at hand. An operating system kernel isn’t trying to create quarterly finance reports. It’s simply trying to help applications utilize the computer as optimal and easy as possible. It’s directly controlling the computer itself so it seems fitting to use a language where how a computer works is the domain area. Thus, while you would write your business logic in Java, Python, or Haskell, you wouldn’t write the virtual machine (VM) and just-in-time (JIT) compiler of said high-level language using that high-level language. You would use a low-level language to optimally utilize the hardware to maximize the abilities of the VM and JIT compiler. In using a low-level language, you are at the level of bits and bytes. You are dealing with all things binary. You are in the heart of Boolean algebra.

And that is where the action is at. Boolean algebra is another thing that for reasons unknown releases endorphins in my brain. When I write that I am a coder that encodes to the computer and decodes from the computer this is what I mean. I take our mental model of how the world works, encode it into a Boolean language, apply Boolean algebra, and decode the result back into our mental model. I do this, all of us programmers do this, because this is how the computer works. The CPU is inherently a Boolean engine. But it goes beyond that. It’s how any digital technology works because of the problem of how to represent information. In nature we only have two stable states that we can reliably discern at all times. High voltage or low voltage, magnetic north-sourth or south-north, light on or light off, dash or dot, true or false, yes or no, and so on. It’s all ones and zeros in the end. Programming in ones and zeros is about as close as I can get to how the universe creates life forms with the compounds A, C, G, and T. The program of life is a string of A, C, G, and T compounds much like our computer programs are a string of ones and zeros. For some reason this excites me and makes me want to be closer to the mathematics of Boolean, not further from it.

Programming languages are ultimately a language for coding (encoding and decoding). In being a coder, I will always have a tether to the computer itself. I may use a high-level language when it is best to do so and I may use a low-level language when it is best to do so. In any case, I am not talking to the computer in pure English. I’m not sure I want to. Regardless of how good or bad a programming language is (and to be clear it’s good or bad in a given context), it will usually be a language construct that is unambiguous (or at least the languages I choose to write code). This unambiguity is vital in understanding software systems. Each line of code is clear in what the CPU will do. We may have our own misunderstanding but that is on us and irrelevant to the topic. The CPU sees an instruction and doesn’t have to wonder what out intent is. It does the instruction as advertised. I will make the claim that English (or any spoken natural language) would make for a terrible programming language because English is ambiguous. The vernacular English is riddled with sentences that can be interpreted in different ways. This is the reason why lawyers have a very particular style of writing English. It is to avoid ambiguity. It’s why a legal document is torturous to read. It’s what it takes to capture a clear unambiguous meaning.

So no, I don’t think English would make the best programming language. And that segues to the next topic of large language models.

Large language models

I am a coder. I enjoy both building software and delivering software to the customer.

I am going to write something and I will do my best to be careful in how I describe my thoughts because I don’t want anybody to walk away from this article thinking I’m a technology luddite. I have no issue with using large language models (LLM), to which I’ll simply refer to as AI, to cut short the journey and get to the destination. I have no issue with an employer expecting me to become a prompt engineer and letting the AI write all the code. I have no issue with other software developers doing this. I’m being sincere. I acknowledge the productivity gains (and they are real).

OK, now I will state something that is true about me. Let’s suppose I have an idea for an application. I convey the idea to the AI via prompt engineering. The AI generates the code. There will be review, some revisions, and then I am done. The idea is now a reality and in record time. Awesome, yes? This does nothing for me. There is no release of endorphins in my brain. This is the truth of how I feel. The journey has been removed from the equation. There is only the destination. I think the problem here is that I am not the restaurant customer. I am the chef. To watch the sci-fi device produce the completed dish instantly does nothing for me as a chef.

I am a coder. It’s not enough to produce a program (the destination). I have to build it myself (the journey). Having the AI spit it out does nothing for me. And, to be very clear, I’m not calling for a revolt against AI. If society wants me use AI to develop software I will. At the end of the day, I need a paycheck to pay my bills. I will give the market what it wants. When I leave the office, I will do what I want. Maybe I’ll hang out with friends. Maybe I’ll play a video game. Maybe I’ll watch a movie. Maybe I’ll tinker on the computer, writing a program for fun. When I do so I will write all the code because that is what is fun for me. If I put up an open-source project that I wrote perhaps now you will understand why I didn’t use AI to do any of the work.

I am a coder.
