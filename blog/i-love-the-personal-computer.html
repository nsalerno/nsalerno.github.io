I love the personal computer

It's personal

I love the programmable personal computer. What else is there to say? Whether it’s the IBM PC, the Commodore 64, Tandy, Amiga, or an Apple computer, I like having a computer that I can program. I like writing the instructions, I like seeing those instructions make the computer come to life, and I like making the computer do something useful.

In general, I like the microprocessor and any computing device built around the microprocessor. The important aspect here is that it is personal. Truly personal. Computing has been around for many decades in various forms. Mainframes and time-sharing systems were the norm before the personal computer arrived. In those times you shared computing resources with other people. In some cases, you waited for your turn to enter your programs (e.g., punch cards). Sometimes you were not in the same room as the system itself but instead used a teletype terminal to interact with the system. What distinguishes the personal computer is that you have a complete computing environment all to yourself. You have the CPU, the RAM, the storage, the keyboard, and the display. You can do (almost) whatever you want, limited by your imagination (but also hardware capabilities place limits too).

For those that want build to software to handle large scale data I admit that the personal computer doesn’t cut it. For truly large-scale endeavors you will eventually need to scale up computing power via a data center. But I have no interest in single-handedly writing software to process the entire world wide web or to process terabytes of scientific data. My personal interests are much more down to Earth and down to scale. For all intents and purposes, my hobbies can be done in the confines of today’s personal computer.

Cloud computing

I know what you are thinking. You are thinking that if I have some program in mind, or some algorithm to tinker with, I can just write it out and execute it via the wonders of Amazon Lambda. It reduces (or eliminates) the boilerplate and accidental complexity of systems programming. Furthermore, I can scale it up or down to what is needed (or what I can afford). Sure, this is all true. However, the problem here for me is that any cloud computing service is not personal. It’s owned by other people. They are the landlords, I’m just leasing.

Cloud computing reeks of the old days before the personal computer era. As they say, what is old is new again. To me, cloud computing is the mainframes come back (with a vengeance). I don’t mean to cast this as a negative. Today’s form of computing has very real benefits. The scalability and economics of cloud computing do make sense. Maybe not in every case, there will be some cases where a company decides its better off with private servers on site or in a co-location. But in other cases, it makes the most sense to develop some software, put it in a container, and deploy it to the nebulous computing farm known as the cloud.

But as I stated, none of it is personal. It’s all leased at the end of the day. Computing as a utility, like electric, gas, and water, just doesn’t sit right with me. Again, I don’t mean to cast this as a negative. I’m saying it’s not for me. It all comes down to the concept of personal ownership. Computing as a utility means you are not in control. The service can be turned off at any moment. Sometimes it’s because you didn’t pay your bill. Sometimes it’s that the landlords deem your activity a violation of their terms of service. In any case you are not in control. With a personal computer you are in control (at least to the extent that you can pay your electric bill). The point is that it’s a one-time cost and the hardware is yours to use indefinitely.

Thus, for me it’s the personal computer all the way. Now, in my professional career I work on systems that are designed to deploy to and run in the cloud. I’ve worked for companies whose strategy revolves around cloud computing. This is OK and I will help them in their endeavor because that is what they pay me to do. But when I go home, I go to the personal computer. It’s mine. The keyboard, mouse, monitor, and the box with the CPU, RAM, and storage.

Smartphones and tablets don’t do it for me. Smartphones feel like they are only for consuming content (read-only). Tablets are better, but still, I too used to a full keyboard for typing and large display monitors for screen real estate.

Look, I get it. I’ve become the very thing I thought I would avoid becoming. I’ve become an old dinosaur. I’ve become the next proverbial COBOL programmer. When I was young, I thought I would stay hip to all the latest and greatest IT technologies. Boy was I wrong! Back then I could not have imagined the world we have today.

The computer as a network appliance

Because I am so attached to the personal computer it does sadden me to see it transform in a way that seems counter to its strength. It does concern me that today’s computer needs an internet connection. What was once a luxury now a necessity. I see software that won’t run if it can’t phone home. I do hope this trend does not continue further such that it renders the personal computer inoperable. Or, maybe I just need to get an old 32-bit computer off of E-Bay and use it until the hardware dies. I guess I don’t need a full personal computer per se. All I need is a microprocessor I can program. Perhaps I will eventually wind up attaching a keyboard and monitor to a Raspberry Pi as the PC shrinks away from the consumer market.

I often wonder if the only PC’s left in the future will be workstations for office professionals because all the people just do everything from their smart watch (oh how Dick Tracy would have marveled at what we have around our wrists). Or maybe most computing devices vanish because we now just ask AI to do everything. In the future we won’t even need keyboards, just call out into the air and verbally prompt the AI. I guess Star Trek got that part right about what the future will look like. All you have to say is “Computer” and then prompt the AI. For what it’s worth, Star Trek also had LCARS so maybe there is still a place for someone like me in the future.

Regardless, I will still tinker with the personal computer while I can.
